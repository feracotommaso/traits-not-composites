## EDITOR (Matthias Ziegler)

> I have now received two reviews. Both reviewers see value in the commentary and in the broader debate it addresses. I agree. The manuscript raises an important question: what follows from rejecting a simple reflective interpretation of broad personality traits? In particular, I think the manuscript is right to question whether the failure of a simple common-cause model necessarily implies that Big Five domains should be treated exclusively as composite-formative constructs.
>
> At the same time, I think the manuscript requires substantial conceptual revision before the proposed alternative is sufficiently clear. Some of these issues overlap with the reviewers' comments, but I would like to add several points of my own. In my view, the central issue concerns the hierarchical nature of personality traits and the distinction between psychometric decomposition and causal explanation.

---

### E1. The hierarchical nature of Big Five personality traits needs to become central to the argument

> The manuscript repeatedly discusses whether broad personality domains can be considered “unidimensional.” I think this framing needs to be reconsidered because unidimensionality is meaningful only relative to a particular level of analysis and a specified set of indicators. Cronbach (1951) already cautioned that “Tests divisible into distinct subtests should be so divided before using the formula.” This is directly relevant here. Broad Big Five domains are explicitly hierarchical constructs consisting of distinguishable lower-order facets. A facet may be sufficiently unidimensional with respect to its own items, but a broad domain is not unidimensional in the same sense. Each facet contains variance shared with the higher-order domain as well as reliable facet-specific variance. Indeed, if facets contained no meaningful specific variance, there would be little substantive reason for distinguishing them in the first place.
>
> This point is also closely related to the distinction discussed by Ziegler and Hagemann (2015) between unidimensionality at the level at which a scale is intended to operate and the inappropriate treatment of multidimensional structures as if they were homogeneous. Similarly, Ziegler and Bäckström (2014) emphasize that lower-order components can contain meaningful specific variance that should not automatically be regarded as error. I therefore think the manuscript should move away from a general question of whether “the Big Five are unidimensional” and instead ask a more precise question:
>
> How much variance is shared at the broad domain level, how much remains specific to the facets or narrower traits, and which of these levels is relevant for the substantive criterion under investigation?
>
> This would substantially sharpen the argument.
>
> Importantly, the manuscript already comes close to this position. It argues that hierarchical models should retain substantive variance at the facet, nuance, item, method, and residual levels, and later explicitly advocates hierarchical SEMs that separate domain-level common variance from lower-order specificity. I would encourage you to consider making this variance decomposition—not the more general claim about “distributed common causes”—the organizing principle of the commentary.

### E2. This has direct implications for the proposed modeling strategy

> If the constructs are hierarchical, the appropriate statistical model should also be hierarchical.
>
> When SEM is used, the model should explicitly distinguish at least: variance common to the broad domain, reliable facet-specific variance, and measurement error.
>
> The central substantive question then becomes whether an external variable is associated with the domain-common component, facet-specific components, or both.
>
> If observed-score regression is used instead, I would argue that the facets, rather than a single domain composite, should generally be entered into the model. Their covariance represents the common trait core, whereas their unique portions permit examination of facet-specific relations. The key empirical question then becomes whether the broad common core is sufficient or whether the facets contribute meaningfully beyond it. This is closely related to the bandwidth–fidelity problem and to work asking whether broad personality dimensions or narrower traits are more informative for particular criteria. In this context, I encourage the authors to engage with Moshagen et al. (2021, EJP). Their work is relevant because the interesting question is not simply whether a broad factor “exists,” but whether the common factor or the specific lower-order components carry the criterion-relevant variance.
>
> The analogy with cognitive abilities can also be made more precise. The hierarchical treatment of cognitive ability does not require one to choose between general and specific abilities. Rather, both levels are modeled, and researchers ask which level explains or predicts a particular outcome. This is a central feature of contemporary hierarchical models of cognitive abilities (see, e.g., Schneider & McGrew, 2018). I believe this is a more useful analogy for the present argument than treating the existence of g as evidence for a particular causal ontology.

### E3. Please distinguish much more sharply between psychometric decomposition and causal explanation

> This point is also central to Reviewer 1's critique and, in my view, needs substantial revision.
>
> At several places, the manuscript appropriately characterizes a higher-order factor as a model-based representation of shared variance. For example, the manuscript states that the factor can represent shared variance generated by multiple partly overlapping causes rather than a single mechanism.
>

The domain factor is described as representing the “shared causal background,” and the causal-inference section suggests that conditioning on this higher-order factor may close backdoor paths through shared etiology. That conclusion does not follow from covariance decomposition alone.
>
> A hierarchical factor model can tell us that a set of facets shares variance. It cannot, on its own, tell us why they share variance. Shared causes, reciprocal relations among facets, semantic overlap, response styles, method effects, social processes, or combinations of these mechanisms may generate similar covariance structures. The manuscript itself acknowledges precisely this ambiguity elsewhere.
>
> Thus, I would recommend making the following distinction explicit throughout:
>
> A hierarchical latent model can provide a useful decomposition of shared and specific variance without thereby establishing the causal mechanism responsible for that variance.
>
> This would preserve what I see as the strongest part of your argument while avoiding an overinterpretation of latent variables as identified causal entities.

### R1.0. Sintesi e valutazione generale

> The commentary engages seriously with Grosz (2026), which I call “target article” in the current review. The authors agree with the target article's central negative claim that the Big Five are not five entities that cause the item responses. They also reject the simple reflective model (one common cause, local independence). The disagreement concerns what should replace that model. The authors propose
>
> - that each domain be represented by a higher-order latent variable that "stands in for a constellation of causes whose joint effects on the indicators are approximately proportional" (p. 4).
>
> - that this representation is explanatory whereas a composite is not
>
> - that it is appropriate for confounder adjustment whereas a composite is not; and
>
> - that it is "concrete and already implementable" (p. 21) as a hierarchical SEM.
>
> The question the commentary raises, what licenses the step from "not simple common causes" to "composites," is legitimate, and I would welcome the commentary's publication together with a response by me. As the author of the target article, I have focused mainly on points that can be checked against the two texts and the cited literature.

### R1.1. The commentary argues against claims the target article does not make

> Several passages attribute an explanatory ambition to the composite-formative model: composites are said to be treated as "the preferred ontological interpretation" (p. 12), to be mistaken for "a solution to an explanatory problem" (p. 12), and to take "the explanatory credit of common causes without paying the modeling cost of representing them" (p. 17). The target article states the opposite, repeatedly and explicitly: the composite-formative model "makes no assumptions about causal effects from or on the indicators" and is recommended for descriptive and predictive research only. Big Five composites "are not very helpful when the goal is to investigate causal effects" (see also Table 2). For explanation, theory building, and interventions the target article recommends narrower traits assessed with multiple methods, for which it proposes reflective (common-cause) models (e.g., talkativeness assessed by self-report, informant report, and audio recordings). The "third path" dilemma (p. 17) therefore misdescribes the target article's position: it claims no explanatory credit for composites. It claims that the sources of within-domain covariance are several (shared genetic and environmental influences, direct effects among narrow traits, semantic overlap, response styles) and cannot appropriately be represented by a single latent variable. ### R1.2a. What a reflective latent variable must be

> I agree with the authors that the latent variable in a unidimensional reflective model may itself be produced by many biological and environmental causes, which is exactly what Figure 1 of the target article depicts. What the model does require is that these many causes act on the indicators through a single variable, so that the indicators are conditionally independent given that variable (e.g., Borsboom et al., 2003; Eronen, 2025). Blood glucose is multicausal, as the commentary notes (p. 7), but every cause of blood glucose affects the meter readings through one variable, the concentration of glucose in the blood. Hence, the readings satisfy a reflective model and the target article uses the analogy. Such a variable is an entity in the sense the reflective model requires ("Entity realism about latent variables is needed to motivate the choice for the reflective model over the formative model"; Borsboom et al., 2003). The evidence indicates that no such variable exists for a Big Five domain: genetic and environmental influences act directly on facets and items (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2012; Franić et al., 2014), and interventions move facets and items non-proportionally (Olaru et al., 2024). A set of causes with different effects on different facets is not a common cause of the facets; Cramer et al. (2012), cited on p. 11, discuss and reject exactly this "placeholder for everything we do not (yet) understand" use of a reflective latent variable.


**Note ET:**

### R1.2b. What follows when the latent variable of a reflective model is defined by the shared variance

> If the domain factor has no identity beyond the shared variance of a particular set of indicators, which is what "not posited as an entity" (p. 20) appears to mean, then it is constructed out of the indicators and cannot be their cause: "One cannot claim that A causes B, and at the same time maintain that A is constructed out of B" (Borsboom et al., 2003, p. 217). The practices that distinguish a reflective model from a composite, treating unique variance as error, disattenuating structural relations, and adjusting for the factor as a common cause, then lose their justification: "to argue for a common factor model requires a case to be made that only the shared variance and not the unique variance of items is of interest. We can think of no rationale for such a case that does not hinge on a common cause" (Rhemtulla et al., 2020, footnote 2; similarly Eronen, 2025, on local independence for a summary statistic). The composite-formative model represents this situation accurately: it defines the domain score as a function of the indicators, which is what a shared-variance factor is, without asserting that the score causes them.

### R1.2c. Il "dilemma" (entity vs. composite)

> The commentary therefore faces a dilemma. Either the domain factor refers to a variable through which all shared causes act, in which case it is an entity and the evidence speaks against it; or it is the shared variance of the indicators, in which case it is a composite with particular weights, and the causal uses made of it in the causal-inference section are unavailable. The commentary's own conclusion takes the second horn: latent trait dimensions "belong primarily to the descriptive level" (p. 21), and Big Five domains are "stable, reproducible patterns of covariation among narrower tendencies" (p. 3). A pattern of covariation is a description, and the model that says so is the composite model. A (hierarchical) reflective model, however, asserts that the domain causes the facets and the facets cause the items (that is what the arrows mean; Bollen & Pearl, 2013; Eronen, 2025), and the causal-inference section uses the domain as a cause. Nor does a factor whose only content is that it makes the facets covary explain the covariance; it re-describes it (Borsboom, 2023, compares such trait explanations to Molière's virtus dormitiva; Cramer et al., 2012, note the circularity). If the explanation consists of the mechanisms the commentary lists on p. 3, then explaining means identifying and modeling those mechanisms, which the target article recommends at the level of narrow traits.

### R1.2d. Esempio concreto: reward sensitivity ed estroversione

> A concrete case makes this clear. The commentary grants that "when those causes can be isolated and measured, they can be modeled directly" (pp. 4–5). ### R1.3. "Approximate proportionality" is not an additional assumption but a testable implication, and the available evidence does not support it

> The commentary states that "approximate proportionality is the relevant assumption; mechanistic singularity is unnecessary" (p. 4), without a reference or a definition. The condition, as I understand it, is that if many causes act on a set of indicators only through a single latent variable, their effects on the indicators must be proportional to the loadings. Proportionality is therefore not an assumption to be granted but an implication that can be checked, and the evidence the commentary itself discusses speaks against it: genetic and environmental influences act directly on facets and items rather than only through the domain (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2012; Franić et al., 2014), and an extraversion intervention changed sociability (d = 0.89–1.00) but not assertiveness (d = 0.12–0.15; Olaru et al., 2024). The commentary's own statements, "an intervention rarely changes extraversion as a whole" (p. 18) and facets predicting an outcome in opposite directions (p. 20), are violations of proportionality, and the proposed rescue of the latter, opposing indirect effects through facets, makes the domain's effects entirely mediated by the facets, so that the causally relevant variables are the facets. Finally, proportionality that holds only in the aggregate of many small causes, the situation invoked with the central-limit argument (pp. 5–6), holds for no particular cause or intervention. This is Thomson's (1916) bonds model, in which tests correlate only because they sample overlapping subsets of many elementary causes. It reproduces the covariance structure of a factor model exactly, so that the two cannot be distinguished statistically, and its general factor is the sum of the sampled causes, an index rather than a common cause (Bartholomew et al., 2009; Revelle, 2024). The "distributed common cause" the commentary defends is, when spelled out, this model, and it is a composite model.

### R1.4. The causal-inference claims: the asymmetry between latent and composite adjustment does not hold, and closing the backdoor path is guaranteed only under proportionality

> I agree with the commentary's own statement that "the right adjustment depends on the causal graph, which depends on the theory" (p. 19): for the Big Five, that graph is exactly what is unknown, the commentary offers no theory of it, and misfit does not reveal it (Hayduk, 2014; Rhemtulla et al., 2020). The commentary also claims that "for a distributed latent factor, controlling for the domain-level variance is the appropriate move to close backdoor paths through the shared etiology; for a purely composite score with no declared causal structure, the concern raised by Wysocki et al. applies" (p. 19). This asymmetry cannot be right, because both the composite and the estimated domain factor are weighted sums of the same items (Borsboom et al., 2003, on the "operational latent variable"; Revelle, 2024). The Wysocki et al. (2022) concern therefore applies to both procedures or to neither, and the claim that one of them is "the appropriate move" should be withdrawn. Moreover, even if a domain were a common cause of its facets, adjusting for it would close the backdoor path only for confounders that act on the exposure facet solely through the shared variance. A confounder with an additional facet-specific effect (say, a stronger effect on sociability than on assertiveness) leaves that part of the path open, and the commentary itself expects the causes' effects to be only "approximately proportional" (p. 4). And if facets or items affect one another directly, which the target article names as one source of within-domain covariance, the domain score contains mediators or colliders of the facet's or item’s effect, so adjusting for it can add bias rather than remove it (Wysocki et al., 2022). This is also why the target article advises against using the Big Five, under either measurement model, as variables in research on causal effects (Table 2 of the target article).

### R1.5. The "concrete and already implementable" alternative is not specified, and the proposed failure criterion is uninformative

> The commentary lists tools (facet factors, method factors, correlated residuals, direct dependencies, cross-loadings, network edges, hierarchical structures; pp. 10, 21) but does not specify a model for any Big Five domain: which causes are represented at which level, which residuals are correlated on which theoretical grounds, and what would count as the model's failure. ## REVIEWER 2 (Christopher J. Soto)

### R2.0. Valutazione generale

> This paper comments on Grosz (2026) by arguing that broad personality traits like the Big Five may be best conceptualized as complex networks of partly shared causes, rather than as either single causal entities or formative composites. Overall, I am persuaded by the paper’s key arguments and think it could make a meaningful contribution to the discourse about how to best conceptualize personality traits at different levels of abstraction.
>
> On the one hand, I agree with Grosz that a broad trait like one of the Big Five should not be conceptualized as a single causal entity (as implied by a simple reflective factor in a latent variable model). On the other hand, I agree with the commentary author(s) that (a) many personality psychologists already recognize this,* (b) many contemporary personality trait theories (e.g., Whole Trait Theory, Cybernetic Big Five Theory) conceptualize broad traits as complex networks of partly shared biopsychosocial causes rather than single entities, and (c) this kind of conceptualization can be captured by using more-complex reflective factor models (e.g., multilevel or bifactor models) to represent sets of more-broadly and more-narrowly shared causes. Therefore, the choice between single reflective factors and formative composites is a false dichotomy.
>
> * My favorite classic quote along these lines comes from Saucier and Ostendorf (1999): “A broad factor is not so much one thing as a collection of many things that have something in common.”
